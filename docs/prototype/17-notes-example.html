Differentiation rules

Six rules that turn any combination of elementary functions into its derivative. Four of them gave you no trouble. The quotient rule and the chain rule took longer, so they get the room here.

01

Powers, sums and constant multiples

Passed first time

Bring the power down and lower it by one. A sum is differentiated term by term, and a constant factor stays where it is. A constant on its own goes, since it does not change.

From your session$\dfrac{d}{dx}\,(4x^3 - 2x + 7) = 12x^2 - 2$
$$\frac{d}{dx}\,x^n = nx^{n-1}$$
02

The product rule

Passed first time
$$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$$

Each factor takes its turn being differentiated while the other waits, and the two results add.

The picture is a rectangle with sides $f$ and $g$. When both sides grow a little, the area grows by two thin strips, one for each side. The corner where they meet is a small thing times a small thing, and vanishes.

From your session$\dfrac{d}{dx}\,x^2\sin x = 2x\sin x + x^2\cos x$
03

The quotient rule

Missed twice, then passed
$$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$$

The top's derivative comes first, and the minus sign belongs to the term with $g'$. The order matters here in a way it does not in the product rule, because a difference is not the same both ways round.

You do not have to remember the order. Write the quotient as a product and let the product rule find it:

$$\begin{aligned}\left(f\cdot g^{-1}\right)' &= f'\cdot g^{-1} + f\cdot\left(-g^{-2}g'\right)\\[4px] &= \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}\end{aligned}$$

Where it went wrong

Twice you wrote the numerator the other way round, $fg' - f'g$. That gives an answer of the right size with the wrong sign, which is why it looked right.

Asked
$\dfrac{d}{dx}\,\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$
You wrote
$\dfrac{x^2-1}{(x^2+1)^2}$
It is
$\dfrac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$

What made it click

The minus comes from differentiating $g^{-1}$, so it has to sit on the $g'$.

Once you derived the rule from the product rule yourself, you stopped trying to recall which term came first. Your next answer, on $\frac{\sin x}{x}$, was right.

04

The chain rule

One miss, one long discussion
$$\frac{d}{dx}\,f\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x)$$

Differentiate the outer function and leave its input alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inner function.

Follow a small step in $x$. The inside stretches it by $g'(x)$. The outer function never sees the step you took: it sees the stretched one, and scales it again by its own rate, measured where its input is. Two scalings, one after the other, multiply.

From your session$\dfrac{d}{dx}\,\sin(x^2) = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x$

Where it went wrong

Two things. At first you took the outer derivative at $x$ and asked why the answer was not $\cos(2x)$. Later, in the check, you lost the sign of the inside.

Asked
$\dfrac{d}{dx}\,e^{-x^2}$
You chose
$2x\,e^{-x^2}$
It is
$-2x\,e^{-x^2}$

What made it click

The sine never sees $2x$. It sees $x^2$.

That sentence, from the discussion, settled where the outer rate is measured. The sign was a slip, not a gap: you named it yourself in the detour ("I dropped the minus").

05

Implicit differentiation

Passed first time

When a curve is given by an equation in $x$ and $y$, differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ and treat $y$ as a function of $x$. Every $y$ then brings a $\frac{dy}{dx}$, by the chain rule. Solve for it at the end.

From your sessionOn the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$: $$2x + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$$ At $(3, 4)$ the slope is $-\frac{3}{4}$, at right angles to the radius, as a tangent to a circle must be.
06

Inverse functions

Passed first time

An inverse swaps inputs and outputs, so it swaps rise and run. Its slope at a point is one over the slope of the original at the matching point.

From your session$f(x) = x^3 + x$ has $f(1) = 2$ and $f'(1) = 4$, so $(f^{-1})'(2) = \frac{1}{4}$, without ever writing the inverse down.
$$(f^{-1})'(b) = \frac{1}{f'(a)}\quad\text{where } f(a) = b$$

The same idea gives the two you will meet most:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x &= \frac{1}{1+x^2}\\[6px] \frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\end{aligned}$$
